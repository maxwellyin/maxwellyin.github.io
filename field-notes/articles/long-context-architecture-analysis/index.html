When long-context reasoning becomes a deployment requirement, architecture choice stops being academic. This note compares the main model families through the lenses of capability, throughput, memory, and serving realism.

Evaluated Models and Architectural Paradigms

As large language models continue to scale in parameter count, context length, and application scope, the Transformer ecosystem in practical engineering has increasingly evolved toward a multi-paradigm landscape. Starting from the original full-attention dense Transformer, the field has progressively expanded to include structured attention mechanisms (Hybrid / Linear Attention), expert routing via Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), cross-modal token compression, and diffusion-based language models (Diffusion LLMs) centered on iterative denoising. Despite their architectural diversity, these approaches are all attempting to address the same fundamental question: under current hardware and inference-framework constraints, how can one achieve a better systemic balance among model capability, throughput, and cost? This shift is evident not only in individual model designs but also in broader industrial deployment paradigms. As long-context processing, chain-of-thought reasoning, cross- document retrieval, and code understanding become mainstream requirements, simply scaling dense Transformers is no longer the only viable path forward. Hybrid Attention has begun to demonstrate superior scalability in long-sequence settings; MoE architectures exhibit strong inductive biases along reasoning pathways; token compression techniques break textual length barriers through explicit structural representations; and Diffusion LLMs represent a fundamentally different generation paradigm, exploring trade-offs between semantic compression and generation consistency. Against this backdrop, the present evaluation conducts a systematic comparison across representative architectural routes. By benchmarking different paradigms within a unified evaluation framework, we aim to more accurately identify their practical usability, bottlenecks, and potential upper bounds within the current hardware ecosystem (H100 GPUs with vLLM / HuggingFace inference stacks).

High-Parameter SOTA Architecture Group

This group includes Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct-AWQ, Kimi- Linear-48B-A3B-Instruct, and Minimax-M2. It is primarily used to compare the task performance and inference-efficiency differences of today’s mainstream architectural routes (full attention, hybrid attention, and their variants) at high parameter scales, and to analyze how different vendors make strategic trade-offs in their accuracy–throughput design choices.

Hybrid Attention: Qwen3-Next and Kimi-Linear-48B

Both Qwen3-Next and Kimi-Linear-48B adopt hybrid attention architectures, following a similar pattern in which several layers of linear or structured attention are interleaved with periodic full- attention layers (approximately one full-attention layer for every three linear-attention layers). The key difference lies in the specific design choices within this hybrid framework. Qwen3- Next employs a more conservative combination of the classic Gated DeltaNet Attention (GDA) and standard full attention. In contrast, Kimi-Linear-48B adopts a more aggressive approach, replacing GDA with an improved variant known as Kimi Delta Attention (KDA), and further introducing a compressed form of full attention, namely Multi-Head Latent Attention (MLA). Moreover, Kimi discards RoPE entirely and relies solely on KDA to maintain relative positional information. As a result of these design decisions, Qwen3-Next natively supports a context length of 256k tokens and can be further extended to approximately 1M tokens when combined with techniques such as YaRN. Kimi-Linear-48B, on the other hand, provides native support for 1M tokens but does not readily support further context-length extrapolation beyond this range.

Dense Transformer: Qwen2.5-32B-AWQ and Minimax-M2

Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct-AWQ follows a standard full-attention Transformer architecture and primarily relies on quantization (Activation-aware Weight Quantization, AWQ) to reduce inference cost. In contrast, Minimax-M2 builds upon a full-attention backbone while adopting a large-scale Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) design with a total parameter count of 230B and approximately 10B active parameters per token. This architecture is further optimized for agent-style tasks, emphasizing multi-step reasoning and decision-making behaviours.

Diffusion LLM Paradigm Comparison Group

This group includes inclusionAI/LLaDA2.0-mini-preview, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, Qwen3-14B, and Glyph. It is designed to evaluate diffusion-based LLMs (DLLMs) against Transformer models of comparable scale across multiple dimensions—task performance, inference throughput, context-length constraints, and generation quality—in order to assess the practical usability, limitations, and potential development headroom of DLLMs in real-world reasoning scenarios.

Diffusion Language Model: LLaDA2.0-mini-preview

LLaDA2 is a 16B-scale Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) diffusion-based language model, with an officially supported context length of 4,096 tokens. The context-length limitation of LLaDA2 primarily arises from its block-based diffusion generation paradigm. Prior to inference, the model pre-allocates a fixed-length sequence divided into blocks of a predefined block_length: the prompt is written into the prefix, while the remaining positions are initialized as placeholder tokens (e.g., [MASK]) to be predicted. During generation, decoding proceeds from left to right at the granularity of blocks. For each block, the model performs multiple denoising iterations over the combination of the currently generated prefix and the active block. At each denoising

step, a full forward pass is executed over the entire prefix sequence of length L, after which a subset of tokens within the active block is updated according to a confidence-based policy. While this generation scheme leverages a block_diffusion_attention_mask to enable parallel prediction of tokens within the active block—thereby improving intra-block filling efficiency—it also causes inference complexity to grow approximately quadratically with sequence length, further amplified by the number of diffusion steps per block. In contrast to autoregressive Transformers, which can rely on KV caching to perform incremental forward computation only on newly generated tokens, diffusion language models must repeatedly recompute the entire prefix sequence at every denoising iteration. Moreover, in the current implementation, the sequence length must be fixed prior to inference, lacking the dynamic length extension capability inherent to autoregressive models. Taken together, these factors result in most existing diffusion language model implementations being practically limited to context lengths of roughly 1k–4k tokens, making smooth scaling to long-context regimes such as 16k–128k tokens difficult in the near term.

Dense Transformer Baselines: Qwen3-14B and Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct

Within this comparison group, Qwen3-14B and Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct both adopt a standard dense Transformer with Grouped Query Attention (GQA), use RoPE as the relative positional encoding, and represent the current generation of high-quality autoregressive LLMs in the 10B–20B parameter range. As a next-generation base model, Qwen3-14B incorporates improvements over its predecessor in long-context modeling and inference stability. It natively supports longer context lengths and can be further extended through techniques such as YaRN, making it a suitable representative baseline for modern dense Transformer architectures. In contrast, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct is a mature, instruction-aligned model within the Qwen2.5 family. It demonstrates stable performance across general question answering, dialogue, and reasoning tasks, and can be viewed as a representative of the classic full-attention Transformer paradigm with strong instruction tuning. Both models leverage KV caching to enable incremental computation during autoregressive decoding, resulting in inference complexity that scales linearly with sequence length. As such, they serve as ideal Transformer baselines for comparison against diffusion-based LLMs, helping to disentangle architectural paradigm effects from differences in training quality.

Cross-Modal Token Compression Model: Glyph

Glyph is built upon the multimodal backbone GLM-4.1V-9B-Base and natively supports a context length of up to 64k tokens. Its key innovation lies in rendering text into images and then encoding them with a vision encoder, achieving an effective 3–4× token compression. This process transforms long textual inputs into more compact visual representations, thereby substantially expanding the amount of context that can be processed within fixed computational and memory budgets. As a result, Glyph represents a technological route distinct from both traditional Transformers and diffusion-based LLMs: it extends long-context capability through cross-modal token

compression, leveraging explicit structural information in the visual domain rather than modifying the underlying language modeling paradigm.

Summary

Overall, the models evaluated in this study span three core generation paradigms: Transformer-based architectures (dense and hybrid), Diffusion Language Models (DLMs), and cross-modal token compression. The Transformer family achieves linear scalability through autoregressive decoding and KV caching, making it the most mature and stable pathway for long-context modeling. Hybrid Attention further optimizes this paradigm by improving the trade-offs among throughput, accuracy, and scalability. Diffusion LLMs introduce an innovative generation mechanism, but due to the constraints of block-based diffusion, they currently struggle to achieve comparable performance in long-context scenarios. In contrast, the cross-modal token compression approach represented by Glyph offers a promising alternative for breaking textual context- length bottlenecks. Together, these three paradigms form the comparative foundation of the present evaluation. This framing allows observed differences in task performance, throughput, and memory usage to be more directly attributed to architectural and generative paradigms themselves—rather than to raw parameter count alone—thereby establishing a coherent reference framework for subsequent performance analysis and architectural assessment.

Experimental Design and Performance Evaluation

This chapter presents a systematic analysis of model capabilities across three complementary evaluation dimensions: LongBench-v1 is used to assess retrieval, summarization, reasoning, and cross-segment 1. evidence integration under long-context conditions, serving as a key benchmark for distinguishing architectural paradigms in long-context understanding. Inference throughput and memory footprint evaluate practical inference efficiency and 2. resource requirements from an engineering perspective under identical input lengths, with particular attention to the systemic differences in deployment cost and efficiency among dense Transformers, hybrid attention models, diffusion LLMs, and cross-modal compression models. LM Harness covers a broad range of general-purpose tasks—including reasoning, 3. question answering, knowledge, mathematics, and dialogue—to characterize baseline model capability and alignment behaviour. Together, these three evaluation dimensions characterize model capability boundaries from the perspectives of cognitive performance, engineering efficiency, and general-task competence, forming a comprehensive evaluation framework for comparing architectural paradigms.

LongBench-v1

To systematically analyze behavioural differences among architectural paradigms in long- context settings, this section evaluates models on LongBench-v1 with task-level granularity. The benchmark covers six core capability categories: Retrieval / Counting, Summarization, Single-hop Question Answering, Multi-hop Reasoning, Code / Repository Question Answering, and Document Classification. These tasks correspond to distinct cognitive

pathways, including local information extraction, cross-segment reasoning chain construction, structured signal recognition, and global semantic aggregation. As such, they serve as key indicators for differentiating dense Transformers, hybrid attention models, token compression approaches, and diffusion language models in long-context understanding. Given that LLaDA2-mini supports a maximum context length of approximately 4k tokens, all comparative experiments involving LLaDA were conducted on subsets with input lengths ≤4k tokens for Qwen2.5, Qwen3, and Glyph as well, in order to ensure fairness. For subtasks in which all samples exceeded the 4k limit (e.g., certain long-document retrieval tasks), LLaDA evaluations were omitted and corresponding results were not reported. This strategy avoids artificial penalties caused by truncation and ensures that observed performance differences more accurately reflect architectural capabilities rather than context-window constraints. In the analyses that follow, we not only compare raw task scores across models but also interpret the observed differences from an architectural perspective. These include the information fidelity of dense attention, the selective sparsification strategies of KDA/MLA, the structural signal advantages of visual token compression models, and the inherent limitations of diffusion-based generation in long-context scenarios. The goal of this section is to decompose model behaviour under long-context conditions in a structured manner, thereby providing a conceptual foundation for interpreting subsequent results on inference throughput and LM Harness evaluations.

In long-document retrieval tasks (passage_retrieval_zh/en), Qwen3-Next-80B, Qwen2.5-32B, and Kimi-48B all demonstrate strong performance, with Kimi-48B achieving perfect scores on both retrieval benchmarks. Retrieval tasks primarily rely on cross-paragraph semantic matching and long-range relevance modeling, and these results are well aligned with the architectural characteristics of the models. Kimi’s KDA and MLA exhibit attention patterns that scale closer to linear complexity on long sequences, enabling lower computational cost and more stable focus when modeling paragraph-level salient relationships. Under large-scale long-context inputs, such hybrid attention mechanisms often perform more direct and efficient semantic filtering than dense attention, making Kimi’s perfect retrieval scores unsurprising. By comparison, Qwen3-Next-80B adopts a more conservative hybrid design with a 3×GDA + 1×Full Attention structure, striking a balance between global fidelity and local focus, and thus consistently achieves scores in the 96–100 range. The dense-architecture Qwen2.5-32B maintains solid performance as well, benefiting from its fully connected attention mechanism. In contrast, Minimax-M2 performs noticeably worse. Despite its large total parameter scale of 235B with approximately 10B active parameters, its results lag significantly behind smaller models such as Qwen3-Next-80B and Kimi-48B that are more directly optimized for long- context modeling. This suggests that the large-scale MoE + full-attention combination

employed by M2 does not realize its theoretical advantages in long-document retrieval scenarios. MoE architectures naturally emphasize expert specialization during routing, biasing attention toward semantic and conceptual decomposition in a high-dimensional sparse space. Coupled with training and alignment strategies that prioritize agent-style reasoning and multi- turn interaction, the optimization objective is not focused on precise paragraph-level matching or cross-segment dependency tracking. As a result, M2 fails to translate its parameter scale into proportional gains on tasks that depend heavily on token-level fidelity and paragraph-level semantic alignment. In other words, despite its substantially larger size, M2 does not benefit linearly from scale in long-context retrieval, exposing the limitations of large MoE + full- attention designs for this task category.

In the counting task (passage_count), architectural differences become even more pronounced. Qwen3-Next-80B achieves a perfect score of 10, while dense-attention Qwen2.5-32B also remains at a relatively high level, indicating that architectures which densely preserve token-to- token interactions have a natural advantage in tasks requiring high token-level fidelity. By contrast, Kimi-48B’s KDA/MLA architecture performs more aggressive compression and aggregation of repeated token patterns in long sequences. While such latent representations are beneficial for summarization and retrieval, they weaken the model’s ability to track repeated tokens individually, leading to a reduced counting score of 4.17. Minimax-M2 performs even worse (2.4), continuing the same pattern observed in retrieval tasks: because its training and alignment strategies do not emphasize strict token-level language modeling, it performs poorest on highly literal frequency-counting tasks. For passage_count, LLaDA2-mini obtains a score of 0.0. This result is not caused by context truncation—all samples were strictly filtered to ≤4k tokens—but instead stems from systematic limitations of diffusion-based generation in parsing structured markers, identifying paragraph boundaries, and enforcing output format constraints. This task requires accurate recognition of structured tokens such as <passage> and the strict generation of a single numerical output. LLaDA’s global diffusion decoding is unstable in positional alignment and pattern counting, frequently producing extraneous text or losing paragraph-structure information, making it nearly impossible to generate valid outputs for this evaluation. Qwen3-14B performs slightly worse than Qwen2.5-14B, primarily due to generation instability: in multiple samples, Qwen3-14B terminated early after producing partial reasoning content, failing to output the final numerical answer. In comparison, Qwen2.5 exhibits stronger instruction alignment and more concise outputs, resulting in higher scores on this task. Glyph delivers the strongest performance overall. Its core advantage arises from its unique input representation: long text is rendered into images and processed by a multimodal encoder. This visualized input explicitly exposes paragraph boundaries, indentation, delimiters, and numbering, allowing the model to more reliably identify structural boundaries and repetition patterns, and to produce numerically precise outputs with higher format stability. Consequently, Glyph is naturally well suited for structure-sensitive tasks such as passage_count, significantly outperforming same-scale pure-text Transformers and diffusion language models.

Overall, Qwen2.5-32B and Kimi-48B maintain leading performance across most subtasks, while Qwen3-Next-80B and Minimax-M2 perform slightly worse, though still within the same general performance band. Summarization primarily requires cross-paragraph semantic compression, global information filtering, and the preservation of key content. As a result, dense attention and hybrid attention structures with strong semantic stratification tend to exhibit more stable advantages. The dense-attention architecture of Qwen2.5-32B performs consistently well on long- document summarization tasks, as full attention preserves complete token-level mutual information, allowing the model to aggregate semantics without losing fine-grained details. Consequently, it achieves relatively higher scores on tasks such as samsum, gov_report, and vcsum. Although Kimi-48B has fewer parameters than Qwen3-Next-80B, its KDA/MLA hybrid attention provides more efficient focus over long sequences, enabling the model to rapidly identify salient information and skip noisy segments through structural sparsification. By comparison, Qwen3-Next-80B does not exhibit a pronounced advantage in summarization tasks. Its 3×GDA + 1×Full Attention design is highly effective for long-context reasoning and cross-segment dependency modeling, but summarization relies more heavily on global semantic compression and information filtering than on deep chain-of-thought reasoning. As a result, its architectural strengths do not fully translate into superior summarization performance. Minimax-M2 follows a similar pattern observed in other long-context tasks: while its MoE + Full Attention architecture provides advantages in large-scale reasoning and dialogue, its training objectives do not explicitly emphasize information compression for summarization. Consequently, it achieves mostly mid-range performance on gov_report, qmsum, and vcsum, failing to reflect the level of stability one might expect from its large parameter scale. The only task where it shows a clear advantage is narrativeqa, which emphasizes detail retention and local memory—properties that align well with M2’s expert- routing bias. Overall, summarization tasks highlight the structural strengths of dense attention (Qwen2.5) and efficient hybrid attention (Kimi-48B) in information filtering and semantic compression. Although Qwen3-Next-80B and Minimax-M2 possess advantages in scale or reasoning capability, their designs and training objectives are more oriented toward complex reasoning

and dialogue, preventing them from forming a dominant advantage in pure summarization tasks and resulting in performance that is balanced but not outstanding.

In multi-hop question answering tasks, the four models exhibit performance distributions that are strongly correlated with their underlying architectures and training objectives. Qwen3- Next-80B and Minimax-M2 both achieve scores of approximately 65.7 on HotpotQA, clearly outperforming Qwen2.5-32B and Kimi-48B. This indicates that in scenarios requiring cross- paragraph evidence chaining and multi-step reasoning, both hybrid attention (Qwen3-Next) and large-scale MoE architectures (Minimax-M2) possess strong long-range reasoning capabilities. Qwen3-Next’s three-layer GDA plus one full-attention hybrid structure enables efficient local focus while preserving global semantic coherence, making it sensitive to key information along multi-hop reasoning paths. Meanwhile, Minimax-M2’s large-scale expert- routing mechanism more readily activates specialized experts when handling multiple relatively independent evidence fragments, allowing it to achieve performance comparable to Qwen3- Next on HotpotQA. On MuSiQue, Minimax-M2’s advantage becomes even more pronounced, achieving a score of 57.5 and significantly outperforming the other models, while Qwen3-Next and Kimi-48B remain in the middle range. MuSiQue is characterized by multiple short passages, with questions requiring repeated transitions across different segments to gather dispersed evidence. Minimax-M2’s expert-routing mechanism provides a structural advantage in such settings, where evidence is fragmented and semantic spans are large. Different experts can independently model distinct types of evidence, resulting in greater robustness for cross- paragraph multi-hop integration. Moreover, M2’s training strategy is strongly oriented toward agent-style reasoning, tool usage, and stepwise decision-making, making it naturally more likely to activate explicit reasoning paths in tasks with clear chain-of-thought structure—further amplifying its advantage. At the same time, this training bias also explains its previously observed instability on extractive, highly structured, or format-sensitive tasks, reflecting a trade-off across capability dimensions. The results on 2WikiMQA exhibit a markedly different pattern. Minimax-M2 achieves a dominant score of 80.22, substantially outperforming all other models, while Qwen3-Next-80B and Kimi-48B score 67.46 and 57.6, respectively. 2WikiMQA places greater emphasis on entity-centric multi-hop relations, attribute chaining, and cross-topic weak multi-hop reasoning, requiring broad knowledge coverage, accurate entity disambiguation, and stable evidence linkage. In such tasks, the MoE paradigm’s semantic-category-based routing enables more effective decomposition of entities and attributes across experts, yielding a clear advantage for M2. By contrast, hybrid attention models show less stable focus when handling loosely structured cross-entity chains, while dense models are more prone to confusion or evidence omission under larger information spans and frequent topic shifts. Overall, the performance ordering in multi-hop QA differs fundamentally from that in single-hop QA. Minimax-M2 exhibits the most pronounced advantage in multi-hop tasks, driven both by the natural suitability of large-scale MoE architectures for semantic decomposition and by training strategies that emphasize agent-style multi-step reasoning. This strength, however, contrasts sharply with its weaker performance on extractive QA, counting, and other structure-

sensitive tasks, indicating a capability profile that prioritizes reasoning chains and multi-step decision-making at the expense of foundational language modeling, format control, and structural alignment. Qwen3-Next’s hybrid attention performs well on medium-span multi-hop tasks such as HotpotQA, but falls short of MoE-based M2 when evidence spans grow larger or reasoning chains become more complex. Qwen2.5-32B, based on dense attention, shows relatively stable but consistently weaker performance across all multi-hop tasks, while Kimi-48B occupies an intermediate position—slightly behind Qwen3-Next overall, yet still maintaining reasonable multi-hop reasoning capability.

In code-related tasks, the four models exhibit a performance distribution that closely aligns with their underlying attention architectures. The most striking observation is that the hybrid- attention models Qwen3-Next-80B and Kimi-48B significantly outperform the two full-attention models, Qwen2.5-32B and Minimax-M2, on both benchmarks. This stratification is not directly correlated with parameter scale; rather, it is determined by how well the attention mechanism matches the structural properties of code-centric tasks. Unlike general natural language tasks, code understanding primarily hinges on integrating long- range dependencies across files, functions, and variables, as well as explicitly recognizing program structure such as call graphs, scope hierarchies, import relations, and control-flow transitions. When faced with such highly structured and redundancy-heavy inputs, dense full attention becomes a liability: treating all tokens as equally connected makes it difficult for attention to concentrate on critical dependency chains, which are easily diluted by redundant context. Moreover, the lack of structural sparsity in full attention prevents models from reliably extracting key semantic flows in long code sequences. This limitation is particularly evident in the results of Qwen2.5-32B and Minimax-M2, both of which suffer substantial performance degradation on repobench-p and lcc, with lcc scores as low as 15.95 and 9.01, respectively— far below those of the hybrid models. In contrast, the hybrid-attention architectures of Qwen3-Next and Kimi-48B demonstrate clear advantages on code tasks. Attention mechanisms such as GDA, KDA, and MLA reorganize attention in a sparsified manner, enabling the model to focus on semantically critical local structures while preserving accessibility to long-range dependencies across segments. For tasks that require tracking cross-file call chains, variable scope transitions, or inter-statement logic, this “local focus with long-range retention” pattern substantially improves the precision of structural reasoning. As a result, the two hybrid models achieve decisive leads on both benchmarks: Qwen3-Next-80B reaches 71.46 on lcc, while Kimi-48B leads repobench-p with a score of 54.64. Overall, results from code-related tasks clearly indicate that hybrid attention is currently the most effective architectural pathway for handling medium- to long-range code sequences, cross-file dependencies, and structured program semantics. Full-attention architectures— whether dense or MoE-based—face consistent structural limitations in this domain. This divergence is not incidental but arises from the intrinsic compatibility between the graph-like semantic structure of code and sparsified attention mechanisms.

By rendering code as images, the multimodal encoder can directly perceive explicit structural signals such as indentation levels, bracket nesting, scope boundaries, cross-file references, and overall layout. For tasks like lcc and repobench-p, which heavily depend on structural consistency, visual encoding significantly reduces the difficulty of long-range dependency tracking and structural parsing, allowing Glyph to maintain exceptionally high stability in cross-scope, cross-function, and cross-file reasoning. In code-centric tasks, the limitations of dense full attention are further amplified. When faced with highly structured, redundancy-heavy code sequences with complex dependency chains, the model struggles to maintain stable focus. Additionally, Qwen3-14B’s generation behavior in structured tasks is more prone to explanatory verbosity, format drift, or incomplete outputs, making it particularly ill-suited for code tasks that demand precise structural parsing. In summary, Glyph exhibits the most pronounced advantage in code-related tasks, as its multimodal encoding directly captures hierarchical code structure and cross-segment relationships, enabling it to significantly outperform all pure-text models. Dense full-attention architectures (Qwen2.5 and Qwen3) struggle to establish stable structural dependency chains in long code sequences, leading to constrained overall performance—moderate for Qwen2.5 and weaker for Qwen3 due to output instability in structured settings. While LLaDA2-mini can leverage formatting regularities in code to remain partially effective, its lack of autoregressive causal-chain modeling imposes a fundamental bottleneck in cross-scope tracking, structural integration, and long-range dependency reasoning. Ultimately, code-centric benchmarks primarily evaluate a model’s ability to represent program structure and cross-dependency graphs, rather than general language modeling or natural language reasoning capability.

In document classification tasks, performance differences across models primarily stem from how strongly each architecture emphasizes global semantic aggregation. Unlike single-hop QA, which relies on localized evidence extraction, classification requires constructing a compact and stable global semantic representation of the entire document, making it particularly sensitive to the integrity of long-range token interactions. Qwen3-Next achieves the best performance on both classification tasks, attributable to its 3×GDA + 1×Full Attention with RoPE hybrid attention design. This architecture preserves a complete global-attention pathway while introducing partial sparsification for efficiency, enabling consistent topic aggregation across full documents. In contrast, Kimi-Linear adopts a more aggressive sparsification strategy (KDA + MLA + NOPE), which compresses, filters, and remaps tokens along the reasoning path. This substantially weakens long-range semantic interactions, resulting in less stable document-level embeddings and the weakest performance in classification tasks. Qwen2.5-32B maintains an upper-mid-level performance through dense full attention, while Minimax-M2 shows relatively limited classification capability, as MoE routing can be unstable for long-tail categories. Overall, classification tasks depend more on the quality of global representations than on local reasoning ability, giving architectures with higher structural fidelity —such as Qwen3-Next—a natural advantage.

Under the same LongBench inference scripts and input configurations, we measured throughput and memory usage for four models with different architectures and scales. In these experiments, Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B, Kimi-Linear-48B, and Qwen2.5-32B-AWQ were deployed on a 4×H100 setup, while Minimax-M2 was evaluated on 8×H100 GPUs. Although the deployment scales are not identical across models, the results are sufficient to reveal their core performance trends under long-context workloads. From the perspective of prefill throughput, architectural differences directly determine efficiency under long-input conditions. Structured-attention models such as Kimi-Linear-48B and Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B achieve approximately 58k–67k tokens/s during prefill, significantly outperforming Qwen2.5-32B-AWQ, which relies on traditional dense attention and AWQ compression and reaches only about 26k tokens/s. Linear or structured attention mechanisms (e.g., Kimi’s GDA/MLA) effectively avoid the quadratic overhead of global attention on long sequences, providing a natural speed advantage when processing long documents. In contrast, Minimax-M2 achieves only around 6.3k tokens/s in the prefill stage. This reflects its more compute-intensive attention pathways for long sequences: even with an 8-GPU deployment, the architecture is not explicitly optimized for ultra-long inputs. During the decode stage, performance gaps persist but are less pronounced than in prefill. Kimi-Linear-48B and Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B continue to lead, with single-token decoding speeds of approximately 200–220 tokens/s, indicating mature implementations of KV-cache access, routing, and tensor parallelism. Qwen2.5-32B-AWQ decodes at around 160 tokens/s, benefiting from reduced bandwidth usage due to AWQ quantization but constrained by the inherent latency of a 32B dense architecture. Minimax-M2 decodes at roughly 70 tokens/s, trailing the other models; this aligns with its larger memory footprint and more complex attention mechanisms, which introduce additional generation latency. In terms of memory usage, Kimi-Linear-48B, Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B, and Qwen2.5-32B-AWQ each consume approximately 300–315 GiB of GPU memory, consistent with their tensor- parallel layouts, KV-cache sizes, and, in the case of Qwen2.5, AWQ’s memory compression. Minimax-M2 requires approximately 633 GiB, nearly double that of the other models. This higher memory demand helps explain its lower decoding throughput: larger KV caches, more complex activation spaces, and additional experts or attention pathways substantially increase memory pressure and reduce on-device data-transfer efficiency. These results indicate that long-context inference performance is primarily governed by the attention mechanism and KV-cache scale, while increasing hardware resources can only partially compensate for architectural complexity. For real-world applications that frequently process long contexts, linear or structured attention mechanisms currently offer the most cost- effective solution.

In the mid-scale experiments, the four models exhibit pronounced differences driven jointly by inference frameworks, deployment scale, and generation paradigms. These differences should not be interpreted as simple measures of model “efficiency,” but rather as manifestations of system-level structural divergence across paradigms. LLaDA2-mini is the only model that cannot be deployed with vLLM and must instead run on the native HuggingFace inference stack, using a single H100 GPU. This setup forces decoding to rely on the multi-step iterative denoising process intrinsic to diffusion language models, preventing the use of key accelerations common to autoregressive models, such as KV caching, paged attention, or continuous batching. As a result, its decoding throughput of 3.03 tokens/s is not anomalously low but reflects the natural performance of the diffusion paradigm under a non-optimized inference framework. Its memory footprint remains modest at 66 GiB, since diffusion models do not require long-lived attention-state caches and primarily store static layer weights and latent representations. In stark contrast, the three autoregressive models—Glyph, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen2.5-14B— are all deployed on 4×H100 GPUs and executed using vLLM’s highly optimized inference pipeline. Features such as PagedAttention, asynchronous decoding, unified KV-cache management, and continuous batching enable substantially higher throughput than traditional frameworks. As a result, these models achieve 20k–70k tokens/s during prefill and sustain over 200 tokens/s during decoding. These figures primarily reflect the strong compatibility between autoregressive Transformers and modern inference optimizations, rather than differences in parameter scale. In terms of memory usage, Glyph consumes significantly more GPU memory than the Qwen models due to its visual rendering and multimodal encoding pathways, which require additional feature maps, cross-modal caches, and explicit structural representations. By contrast, the Qwen models’ memory usage is dominated by large KV caches and Transformer weights. Overall, these results expose fundamental system-level differences among three paradigms. Diffusion-based models struggle to match the highly optimized decoding efficiency of autoregressive models even on comparable hardware; Transformer-based LLMs achieve nearly an order-of-magnitude throughput improvement through vLLM-style system optimizations; and multimodal structured models trade higher memory consumption for stable throughput and enhanced structural reasoning. In this sense, the reported numbers do not simply indicate which model is faster, but rather reflect the distinct efficiency profiles that naturally emerge from different generation paradigms and inference frameworks under similar hardware conditions.

Its attention mechanism preserves high information fidelity at mid-scale parameter sizes, and its mature alignment pipeline yields clear advantages in factual reliability, hallucination control, and pattern stability. Qwen3-14B scores slightly lower than Qwen2.5-14B but exhibits a highly similar capability profile: it remains strong on knowledge, mathematics, and reasoning tasks, yet is more prone to generation instability and explanatory verbosity on evaluations requiring strict formatting or alignment (e.g., TruthfulQA-Gen, HumanEval), which lowers its overall average. Its visual encoding pathway provides advantages in classification-style factual judgment and certain reasoning tasks, particularly when structure is clear and patterns are fixed, allowing it to rapidly leverage visual structural priors. However, on tasks that require text-level reasoning chains, strict token- level consistency, or causally grounded language generation (e.g., HumanEval, TruthfulQA MC2), its structured representations cannot fully substitute for autoregressive language modeling, placing it slightly below dense models in overall ranking. Its primary limitation arises from the lack of causal-chain modeling inherent to autoregressive Transformers, making it difficult to reliably establish cross-sentence dependencies, factual relations, and logical inference paths, and leading to higher susceptibility to semantic drift or incomplete outputs under format constraints. This capability profile is consistent with its weaknesses observed in LongBench, further confirming that current diffusion-based LLMs remain uncompetitive with autoregressive architectures on general reasoning and knowledge- intensive tasks.

Overall, the capability ranking among mid-scale models is clearer than in the large-model group. Dense Transformers remain the most robust general-purpose architecture; Qwen3-14B shows improved reasoning but is limited by output stability; Glyph gains advantages from structural explicitness but is constrained by distortions introduced by non-text generation pathways; and diffusion-based LLMs remain significantly limited on tasks requiring causal chains and strict structural consistency. Compared to the large-model group, these differences are more pronounced at mid-scale parameter sizes, providing important reference points for discussing architectural scalability in subsequent analyses.

Conclusion

This work presents a systematic comparison of multiple architectural paradigms—Dense Transformers, Hybrid Attention, large-scale MoE, Diffusion LLMs, and cross-modal token compression—across three evaluation dimensions: LongBench, inference throughput and memory footprint, and LM Harness. The main conclusions can be summarized as follows. First, Dense Transformers remain the most robust general-purpose solution in the mid- parameter regime. Full-attention architectures represented by the Qwen2.5 series exhibit consistent and stable performance across knowledge understanding, factual consistency, summarization, and classification tasks, making them a reliable foundation for general baselines and downstream applications. However, as model scale and context length increase, dense architectures encounter clear bottlenecks in computation cost, memory consumption, and long-range information modeling, making it difficult to sustain the same advantages at larger scales. In contrast, Hybrid Attention demonstrates superior performance–efficiency–scalability trade-offs at large model scales and in long-context settings. Leveraging structured attention mechanisms such as GDA, KDA, and MLA, Qwen3-Next and Kimi-Linear consistently outperform dense Transformers on long-text retrieval, single-hop and multi-hop reasoning, and cross-file code understanding, while also offering more favorable prefill/decode throughput and memory efficiency. Under current hardware constraints, Hybrid Attention is no longer merely a compromise, but rather a preferable architectural solution for large-scale long-context models, with clear system-level potential. Third, large-scale MoE architectures exhibit strong reasoning-amplification characteristics. Minimax-M2 achieves decisive advantages in multi-hop reasoning and chain- of-thought tasks; however, these gains come at the cost of weaker performance on extractive QA, counting, code understanding, and structure-sensitive tasks, as well as significantly higher memory consumption and lower throughput. Meanwhile, Diffusion LLMs in their current form remain exploratory. LLaDA2-mini reaches moderate-to-strong performance on tasks emphasizing global semantic compression, such as summarization, but shows systematic weaknesses in counting, multi-hop reasoning, structured QA, and code-related tasks. In addition, the lack of KV-cache support and compatibility with mainstream inference frameworks leads to substantially lower decoding throughput and limited context scalability compared to autoregressive Transformers. At present, diffusion-based LLMs are better viewed as a research direction rather than a practical replacement for Transformers in production environments.

Finally, cross-modal token compression offers a promising “structural explicitness” pathway. By rendering long text into images and modeling them with a multimodal encoder, Glyph achieves substantially stronger performance than same-scale text-only models on structure-sensitive tasks such as counting, code understanding, multi-hop reasoning, and classification. These gains come with higher memory costs and partial degradation in pure text causal modeling. Overall, this approach is better suited as a complementary solution for extremely long contexts and strong structural demands, rather than a full replacement for text- based Transformers. Taken together, these results indicate that Dense, Hybrid, MoE, Diffusion, and cross-modal token compression architectures each occupy distinct capability boundaries and engineering trade-offs. Looking ahead to model design for long-context and complex reasoning scenarios, continued optimization of dense and hybrid architectures for long-range reasoning, structural modeling, and throughput efficiency remains essential. At the same time, exploration of MoE, diffusion-based models, and cross-modal compression should continue, with clearer delineation of their application domains and system roles, in order to achieve a balanced trade-off among performance, cost, and robustness at the system level.
